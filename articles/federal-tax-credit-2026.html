Solar Tax Credit 2026: What Actually Changed After the 30% Credit Ended

If you are reading that a 30% federal solar tax credit is still available in 2026, that information is wrong — and a large share of the solar websites still publishing it have never updated their calculators or guides. H.R.1, signed into law on July 4, 2025, cancelled the residential solar tax credit nearly seven years ahead of the original Inflation Reduction Act schedule, so systems installed on or after January 1, 2026 receive $0 federal credit for cash and loan purchases. This guide explains exactly what changed, what still exists for commercial and leased systems, why the old 30% figures persist online, and — most importantly — how the real 2026 numbers still make solar a paying investment in most locations.

The United States residential solar tax credit — Section 25D of the Internal Revenue Code — let homeowners deduct 30% of a purchased system's cost from their federal taxes. Under the Inflation Reduction Act, that 30% rate was supposed to hold through 2032 and then step down gradually to zero by 2035. On July 4, 2025, H.R.1 (the "One Big Beautiful Bill") was signed into law and rewrote that schedule: the residential credit now ends for expenditures made after December 31, 2025, with no phase-down. A system that is fully installed on or after January 1, 2026 qualifies for nothing at the federal level if it was bought with cash or a loan. That single legislative change rewrites the payback math for every American homeowner considering solar today — and almost nobody online has updated their numbers.

This guide is written for a global audience because the lesson matters everywhere: incentives change, often overnight, and the websites that still quote the old rates will quietly cost you real money. We cover the verified 2026 facts, the commercial and lease carve-outs that survived, the depreciation changes businesses should know about, and the calculator-driven approach to re-running your numbers without any federal credit.

What the law actually says: verified 2026 facts

H.R.1 amended Section 25D so the credit expires for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. Critically, 26 USC 25D(8)(A) defines when an expenditure is "made": it is treated as made when the original installation is completed — not when you sign the contract or hand over payment. A homeowner who paid in full in 2025 but had their system installed in January 2026 does not qualify. The safe line is simple: the system must be running by December 31, 2025. Unused credit from a qualifying 2025 installation still rolls over to future tax years, but new installations get nothing.

Rule Before H.R.1 (IRA schedule) 2026 reality
Residential credit (Sec. 25D) 30% through 2032, then 26% / 22% / 0 $0 for systems installed on/after Jan 1, 2026
When the credit counts as "spent" Same rule applied Installation completion date — payment date does not matter
Commercial / lease / PPA (Sec. 48E) 30% until 2035 30% through Dec 31, 2027; construction start by Jul 4, 2026 extends to 2030
Depreciation (business systems) Section 179 / MACRS 5-year 100% first-year bonus depreciation, permanent
Component origin (48E, 2026) None At least 40% of value must be non-FEOC, rising 5% per year to 60% by 2030

The commercial picture is very different from the residential one. Section 48E — used by commercial projects, leases and power purchase agreements — keeps the 30% credit for systems placed in service by December 31, 2027, and any commercial project that began construction by July 4, 2026 can qualify through 2030 as long as it is placed in service within four years. Storage projects are exempt from the placed-in-service deadline entirely. Add a new permanent 100% bonus depreciation that replaced the old 5-year MACRS schedule, and business-owned solar actually gained financial tools in 2026 even as homeowners lost theirs.

Why most competitor sites are still wrong

Search "solar tax credit 2026" and a majority of the top results still promise a 30% federal credit through 2032 — the old Inflation Reduction Act schedule that H.R.1 cancelled in July 2025. The reason is inertia: most solar content is written once and never re-audited, and lead-generation sites have no financial reason to tell you the incentive you are hoping for no longer exists. The practical cost of this is concrete — a system quoted at "$28,160 before the 30% credit, so about $19,700 after" is now a $28,160 system, full stop. That is an $8,400 gap between what an outdated calculator tells you and what your tax return will actually show.

The tell-tale signs of a stale page are easy to spot: any mention of "30% through 2032," phase-down years of 26% (2033) and 22% (2034), or a calculator that subtracts the federal credit from your quote without a date check. Before you trust any solar savings figure in 2026, check whether the page acknowledges that the residential credit ended December 31, 2025. If it does not, its payback numbers are optimistic by roughly 30% of the system cost.

The math without the credit: what still makes solar pay

Losing $9,000 of average federal savings sounds decisive, but three forces kept working in solar's favor through 2026. Equipment prices hit record lows: the average residential solar price fell to about $2.50 per watt in late 2024, down from more than $3.80 per watt a decade earlier. Retail electricity keeps climbing: utility rate growth of 4-6% per year in many regions makes every self-generated kWh worth more each year, and AI-driven data center demand is pushing utilities to plan expensive capacity additions whose costs land on ratepayers. The credit was never the whole story: state rebates, utility net metering or net billing programs, sales tax exemptions and local incentives still stack underneath the federal layer.

The honest payback comparison looks like this: a typical 11 kW system at $2.50/W costs about $27,500 before incentives. With the old 30% credit, average payback ran 8-10 years in high-rate states; without it, the same system lands around 12-18 years depending on electricity price and net metering treatment. What homeowners should actually do in 2026

First, stop using any calculator that subtracts a federal credit — including most on this topic's competitor pages. Second, get your actual numbers into a credit-free model: your annual kWh usage, your retail rate, your roof's production estimate (our energy production calculator handles this), and any state or utility incentive that still applies in your area. Third, compare three purchase routes on total 20-year cost, not on monthly payment: cash, loan and lease/PPA — note that leases remain attractive precisely because the leasing company claims the surviving Section 48E credit and can pass part of it through, which is a structural advantage that did not exist before the residential credit died. Fourth, check your net metering rules, because a state that moved from net metering to net billing changes your battery decision more than any incentive does; the bill savings calculator and the country-by-country net metering guide cover both.

Field note: In markets where the federal residential credit just died, the installer landscape changes within two seasons: national sales-driven installers pull back first, and the quotes that remain come from smaller crews whose prices reflect real labor, not subsidized volume. During the rush to install before a deadline, workmanship slips — undersized conduit, untorqued lugs, panels mounted over wet flashing. If you are evaluating a 2026 quote, the absence of a federal credit is actually a filter: companies still winning projects on pure economics tend to be the ones who size systems correctly and survive on referrals. Ask for a completed reference install of the same size, and make the quote include the exact inverter model, wire gauge and mounting method. Common tax credit questions, answered

"Can I claim the credit if my system was commissioned in 2025 but I have not filed my taxes yet?" Yes — the installation completion date is what matters, and you can roll any unused portion of the credit into future tax years.

"Do battery systems still qualify for anything?" Owned (cash or loan) batteries installed after December 31, 2025 get no federal credit, same as solar. Batteries attached to a commercial or leased system under Section 48E still qualify, and standalone storage has no placed-in-service deadline under the new law.

"What about state solar incentives?" State rebates, property tax exemptions and utility programs are untouched by H.R.1 — some states even expanded programs in 2026 to partially replace the lost federal layer. Check your state energy office and utility site directly rather than trusting aggregator pages.

"Will Congress bring the residential credit back?" Nobody can guarantee it. Legislation restoring or extending residential credits has been proposed, but banking on it means delaying your decision on an uncertain timeline while electricity rates keep compounding — model both scenarios with real numbers and let the payback decide.

Frequently asked questions

Is there still a federal solar tax credit in 2026?

For homeowners buying with cash or a loan, no — the Section 25D credit expired for systems installed on or after January 1, 2026. Commercial systems, leases and PPAs keep the Section 48E credit (30%, through 2027, extendable to 2030 with an early construction start).

Why do some websites still say the solar tax credit is 30% through 2032?

They are repeating the pre-H.R.1 Inflation Reduction Act schedule, which the July 4, 2025 law cancelled. Any page still advertising a 30% residential credit for 2026 is outdated, and its savings estimates will be roughly 30% of system cost too high.

Does solar still pay for itself without the federal credit?

Yes in most cases — record-low equipment prices around $2.50/W, rising retail electricity rates and surviving state programs keep paybacks in the 12-18 year range for typical systems, versus 8-10 years with the old credit. Run your own numbers with a credit-free calculator.

Can I still get the credit if I paid for my system in 2025 but it was installed in 2026?

Almost certainly not. Under 26 USC 25D(8)(A), an expenditure is treated as made when the original installation is completed, so the system must be fully installed by December 31, 2025 to qualify — the payment date is irrelevant.

What tax incentives still exist for solar in 2026?

Commercial projects and leases/PPAs keep the 30% Section 48E credit plus 100% first-year bonus depreciation and a 40% non-FEOC component floor in 2026. Homeowners keep state rebates, net metering where available, sales tax exemptions and local utility programs — only the federal residential credit is gone.

Is a lease or PPA better than buying now that the credit is gone?

Leases and PPAs offer zero-down access because the company claims the surviving commercial credit, but you sign 20-25 year contracts and give up ownership savings. Compare total 20-year cost of lease monthly payments against a purchased-system quote at your local electricity rate before deciding.
