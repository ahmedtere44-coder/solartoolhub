How to read a solar panel datasheet

Every good system starts with the datasheet. Before you buy or install a single panel, its specification sheet tells you the voltage, current, power and temperature behaviour you need to design safely. Here's what each number means — and how to actually use it.

A solar panel datasheet can look intimidating: two columns of numbers, a few acronyms, and a couple of graphs. But it's really just the panel's ID card. Learn to read a handful of key figures and you can size strings, match an inverter, and avoid the mistakes that damage equipment. Skip it, and you're designing blind.

First: everything is measured at STC

Almost every headline number on a datasheet is measured at Standard Test Conditions (STC): 1000 W/m² of irradiance, a 25 °C cell temperature, and an air mass of 1.5. This is a lab benchmark, not your roof. On a hot day the cells run far above 25 °C; on a cold morning they run below it. That single fact is why the temperature coefficients (further down) matter so much — they translate the lab numbers into real behaviour.

Power: Pmax and tolerance

The maximum power (Pmax) — for example 550 W — is the panel's nameplate rating at STC. Next to it you'll often see a power tolerance like 0 / +5 W, meaning the panel will produce at least its rated power and possibly a little more. Pmax is what people quote when they say "a 550-watt panel," but on its own it tells you nothing about how to wire it — for that you need the voltage and current figures.

The four numbers that matter most: Voc, Vmp, Isc, Imp

These four are the heart of the datasheet:

  • Voc — open-circuit voltage. The voltage with nothing connected. It's the highest voltage the panel reaches, and it's the number you use to make sure a string won't exceed your inverter's maximum input voltage.
  • Vmp — voltage at maximum power. The operating voltage under load, at the maximum power point. Always lower than Voc. Strings of Vmp must land inside the inverter's MPPT window.
  • Isc — short-circuit current. The current with the output shorted — the highest current the panel produces. Used for sizing fuses, breakers and cable.
  • Imp — current at maximum power. The operating current under load. Vmp × Imp = Pmax.

In short: the mp values (Vmp, Imp) describe normal operation, while Voc and Isc describe the extremes you design your protection and limits around.

Temperature coefficients: the make-or-break spec

Because the panel almost never sits at 25 °C, the datasheet gives three temperature coefficients — for power (Pmax), voltage (Voc) and current (Isc). They're small percentages per degree, and they carry a sign:

  • Coefficient of Pmax (e.g. -0.34%/°C): power drops as the panel heats up. This is the one that quietly steals summer output.
  • Coefficient of Voc (e.g. -0.27%/°C): voltage rises as the panel gets colder. This is the safety-critical one — on a cold morning, string voltage climbs above the STC figure, and a string that looked fine at 25 °C can exceed the inverter's maximum voltage and damage it.
  • Coefficient of Isc (small, positive): current rises very slightly with temperature.

This is exactly why the string sizing calculator asks for your coldest expected temperature: it uses the Voc coefficient to check the worst-case high voltage, so your string stays inside safe limits year-round.

Field note

Before installing any system, you have to read the datasheet first. The numbers on it — the current, the voltage, and the standard test conditions they were measured at — are what you actually design around. You choose the string length, the inverter and the protection based on those figures, not on guesswork. In the field, the datasheet is the starting point for the whole design: read it properly and the rest of the system falls into place; ignore it and you end up with a mismatched or unsafe install.

NOCT: a more realistic operating point

Many datasheets also list ratings at NOCT (Nominal Operating Cell Temperature, usually around 44–45 °C) as well as at STC. NOCT figures are lower than STC because they reflect a warmer, more realistic cell temperature. They're a useful sanity check on what the panel really does once it's on a roof in the sun.

Efficiency and mechanical data

The module efficiency (e.g. 21%) tells you how much of the sunlight hitting the panel becomes electricity — higher efficiency means more watts from the same roof area, which matters when space is tight. Further down you'll find mechanical details: dimensions and weight (for mounting and roof loading), maximum system voltage (e.g. 1000 V or 1500 V), and the maximum series fuse rating (for protection design).

How the datasheet drives the whole design

Once you can read these numbers, the design follows naturally. Use Voc and its temperature coefficient with the string sizing calculator to set a safe string length; check the operating voltage against your inverter; size protection and cable from Isc with the cable calculator; and feed the panel wattage into the panel calculator and energy production calculator to work out how many panels you need and what they'll generate. Frequently asked questions

What is a solar panel datasheet?

It's the manufacturer's specification sheet for a module — the electrical ratings, the test conditions, the temperature coefficients, efficiency and mechanical details. It's the primary document used to design a safe, correctly sized system.

What do Voc and Vmp mean on a solar panel?

Voc is the open-circuit voltage (maximum, nothing connected); Vmp is the voltage at the maximum power point (operating voltage under load). Voc is higher and is used to keep a string under the inverter's voltage limit, especially in the cold.

Why does the temperature coefficient matter?

It shows how voltage, current and power shift with temperature away from 25 °C. The Voc coefficient is critical: in cold weather voltage rises, so a string can exceed the inverter's limit if you ignore it.

What are STC on a solar panel datasheet?

Standard Test Conditions: 1000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature, air mass 1.5. All the headline ratings are measured there, so real-world figures differ and you apply coefficients and loss factors when designing.
