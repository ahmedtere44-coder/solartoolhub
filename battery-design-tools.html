How to size a solar battery bank the right way

Battery storage is usually the most expensive part of a solar system, so sizing it correctly matters more than any other decision. Work through the same sequence a site engineer would: load, autonomy, depth of discharge, and voltage.

Step 1 — Start from daily energy, not appliance count

Add up the watt-hours each appliance consumes per day (watts × hours used). A fridge at 120 W running 8 compressor hours uses about 960 Wh; lights, TV and phone charging typically add another 300–500 Wh. This total is your baseline daily load in Wh, and it drives every number that follows.

Step 2 — Decide your days of autonomy

Days of autonomy is how many days the battery must keep the system alive with zero solar input. For occasional grid areas, 1–2 days is common; for fully off-grid homes and telecom sites, 2–3 days is the safe engineering default. Multiply the daily load by this number to get usable energy needed.

Step 3 — Size for depth of discharge

Lithium (LiFePO4) banks can safely use 80–90% of capacity, while lead-acid and AGM should stay at 50% depth of discharge to survive 4–6 years. Divide your usable energy by the allowed depth of discharge to get the true bank capacity. A 3 kWh usable need on lead-acid means a 6 kWh physical bank — this is where most budget solar quotes go wrong.

Step 4 — Match voltage and charge controller

Keep small systems (under 1.5 kWh) at 12 V, mid-size (1.5–3 kWh) at 24 V, and anything larger at 48 V. Higher voltage means thinner cables, less heat loss and cheaper hardware. Then size the charge controller so the panel array's max current stays inside its rating with a 25% safety margin.

Tip from the field: never charge a lithium bank with a PWM controller, and always add a BMS to every lithium installation. These two rules prevent the majority of battery-related faults we see in real installations.
