Solar panels in Cyprus

Cyprus has the best solar resource in the European Union — long hot summers, high air-conditioning load, and electricity prices that make rooftop solar one of the fastest-payback investments in Europe. This guide covers sun hours, tariffs, net billing, and realistic costs and payback.

☀️ Peak sun hours in Cyprus by region

Peak sun hours (PSH) is the number of hours per day the sun delivers 1000 W/m². Cyprus averages about 5.5–6.0 PSH — the highest in the EU, comparable to the best sites in the Middle East. The interior (Nicosia) and the south coast (Limassol, Larnaca) are sunniest; the Troodos mountains and the west coast (Paphos) are a touch lower in winter. Summer days deliver 7–8 effective sun hours — which is exactly when the island's AC load peaks. See our guide on peak sun hours by location.

⚡ Electricity tariff and net billing in Cyprus

The Electricity Authority of Cyprus (EAC) charges residential customers roughly ₵0.18–0.26/kWh all-in depending on the block — among the higher prices in the EU — and air conditioning pushes summer bills hard. Cyprus operates net billing: exported energy is credited against consumption at the regulated rate within the billing period, so a well-sized rooftop system can bring the summer bill close to zero. A large share of Cypriot homes already runs solar water heaters (the island is a world leader per capita) — adding PV on the same roof is the natural next step. 💡 Why Cyprus has Europe's fastest solar payback

It is the perfect storm for PV: EU's best sun, high retail prices, strong net billing, and a load profile (AC) that peaks precisely when production peaks. A 5 kW system produces roughly 7,500–8,500 kWh a year — close to a whole household's annual use — and payback commonly lands around 4–6 years, the fastest in Europe. In summer the system can produce more than the home uses, and net billing banks the surplus. Read our guide on solar panels in hot climates — Cyprus is the textbook case: panels lose a few percent in the 40°C heat, so allow a small oversize margin.

🛠️ Field note

Cyprus is what Mediterranean solar looks like at its best — and it teaches the oversize-margin lesson clearly. In July, rooftop temperatures hit 65–70°C and panels lose 10–15% of their rated output to heat; sizing the array 10% larger than the paper minimum recovers that loss cheaply. Second, the island's existing solar water heaters are an advantage, not a redundancy: a home that already heats water with the sun has removed its biggest winter load, so PV covers the remainder (AC, fridge, lighting) with an even smaller array.
